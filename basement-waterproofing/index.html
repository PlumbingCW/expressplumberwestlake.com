Find the source first

Not every wet basement is a waterproofing job. Water can come from groundwater through the walls and floor, from a leaking pipe, from a failed sump pump or from a sewer backup. Each has a different fix, and a coating over the wrong one is wasted money.

We start by looking for the entry points and testing the plumbing. A meter test and a camera inspection rule out pipe causes, and a sump check tells us whether the pump is keeping up. See leak detection and sewer inspection for those steps.

Gutters and grading matter more than most people expect. Downspouts that discharge at the foundation and soil that slopes toward the house send water to the wall. Fixing these often reduces the load on everything else.

Floor sealers: what they can and cannot do

A coat of waterproof floor paint can improve the look of a damp floor, but it will not stop water coming up through concrete. If hydrostatic pressure pushes water through the slab, the paint will blister and peel. Use it only on a floor that is dry, or after drainage has been addressed.

Why sump pumps and drains work together

Interior drain tile collects water at the footing and directs it to a sump pit. The pump then moves it out of the house. That is why a waterproofing plan usually includes a sump pump, sized for the water coming in.

A battery backup is a sensible addition. Wet-weather power outages and flooded basements often go together.

Mold, odors and what to do now

If the basement is wet now, remove wet items and run fans and a dehumidifier. Wet drywall and carpet that stay damp can grow mold within days, and those materials may need to be removed. If the wet area is large or sewage is involved, call a professional for the cleanup.

A simple wet-basement checklist

  1. Note when the water appears: after rain, after snowmelt, or only when you run water in the house.
  2. Look at where it appears, such as the floor edge, a wall crack or around a floor drain.
  3. Check the downspouts and the soil slope outside that wall.
  4. Take photos and a short video. They help any contractor see what you saw.

Water that appears only when you run the washer or flush a toilet points to a drain or sewer problem, not groundwater. Water that appears only after rain points to groundwater or the sump. That one clue changes the entire plan.

Protecting the things you keep downstairs

Store boxes on shelves or plastic risers, not directly on the floor, and keep electronics and important papers upstairs. Cardboard wicks moisture quickly and mold follows. If you plan to finish the basement, fix the water source first, because new drywall and flooring over a damp slab will be ruined.

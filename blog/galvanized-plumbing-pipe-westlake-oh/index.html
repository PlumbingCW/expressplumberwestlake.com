Galvanized Steel Plumbing Pipe in Westlake, OH Homes

Galvanized pipe is steel coated in zinc, and it corrodes from the inside. If your house has it, expect falling pressure, rusty water and eventually leaks. Replacing it is a planned job that is cheaper to do before a pipe fails.

By Westlake Express Plumbing · Published November 2026

Galvanized steel was a standard material for water supply lines in houses built through the middle of the last century. A zinc coating protects the steel from rust, but the coating wears away on the inside, and rust then builds up along the pipe wall. The pipe narrows over time, and the water that passes through it picks up rust and mineral flakes.

The result is a house where the pressure is weak at the upstairs bathroom, hot water is slow, and a tap that has not run in a while delivers brown water. If those symptoms sound familiar, check what the supply pipes are made of before you spend money on fixtures.

How to tell if you have galvanized pipe

TestGalvanized steelCopper or plastic
ColorDull gray, sometimes with rust streaksOrange-brown copper or white, red or blue plastic
MagnetA magnet sticksA magnet does not stick
Scratch with a keySilver-gray metal under the surfaceBright copper color, or plastic
JointsThreaded fittings with visible threadsSoldered, crimped or push-fit joints

Look where the water line enters the house and at the pipes in the basement or crawl space. If you find gray threaded pipe, you have galvanized supply lines in at least part of the house. Note where they run, because a partial replacement may be an option.

Why galvanized pipe fails

Interior corrosion

Rust builds up on the pipe wall and narrows the opening, so flow drops.

Mineral buildup

Scale and rust together choke the pipe, especially in hot water lines.

Failing joints

Threaded joints corrode first and leak at the fittings.

Mixed metals

Where galvanized meets copper, galvanic corrosion eats the steel side.

OptionWhen it makes senseTrade-off

Westlake notes: lead, service lines and who owns what

Cleveland Water says in its published report that some homes and buildings in its service area have lead service lines or plumbing that contains lead. It keeps a service line inventory that you can search by address, and its 2024 lead sampling found 1 of 52 samples above the 15 parts per billion action level, with a 90th-percentile result of 3.15 parts per billion.

Interior galvanized pipe is a separate question from the service line that runs from the street to your meter. Cleveland Water's leak guidance says the utility repairs the line from the street main to the curb stop, and the customer is responsible for the service line from there, including the stretch from the tree lawn to the meter. Check the inventory for your address, then ask the utility what it means for you.

What a replacement involves

We start by mapping the existing pipe runs and deciding on the route for the new ones. In many houses, the basement or crawl space gives open access to most of the lines, and walls are opened only where needed for risers and fixtures. The water is off for part of a day, and the work is staged to restore a bathroom and the kitchen first.

New material is usually PEX, copper or CPVC. Each has a place. PEX is quick to run in existing walls, copper is a long-standing choice for exposed runs and CPVC is a lower-cost option for some jobs. We explain the reasons for our recommendation and listen to your preference.

Permits

Replacing supply piping is plumbing work that generally needs a permit and an inspection, though the rules vary by city. Westlake's city website lists Building Permits and Registered Contractors without describing the plumbing rules, so call City Hall at 440-871-3300 and ask what applies. Keep the permit and inspection record. It is useful when you sell the house.

Before you decide

  • Check the water pressure at several fixtures, not just one.
  • Run the cold tap for a minute after the house has been idle and note the water color.
  • Look at the pipes in the basement for rust, drips and green corrosion at joints.
  • Ask for a clear price in writing, with the scope of drywall repair spelled out.
  • Look up your address on the utility's service line inventory.

Fixing low pressure before you commit to a repipe

Not every low-pressure complaint means failing pipe. A partly closed main valve, a failed pressure regulator or clogged aerators can all choke flow. Check those first, because they are cheaper to fix. If pressure is low at every fixture and the pipes are galvanized, corrosion is the likely cause.

A pressure gauge on an outdoor spigot tells you the incoming pressure. Compare it with the pressure at an upstairs tap. A big drop between the two points to the pipes inside the house.

Living with galvanized pipe while you plan

If a replacement is not in the budget yet, you can reduce the risk. Know where the shutoff valves are, replace corroded fittings as they fail and run the taps now and then so water does not sit. Do not use hot water from a line with heavy rust for drinking or cooking. Keep a list of the symptoms and dates, which helps a plumber give a firm recommendation when you are ready.

Replacement supply piping must meet the Ohio Plumbing Code, which is based on the International Plumbing Code (IPC), so ask what material and fittings your plumber plans to use and whether they are approved for drinking water.

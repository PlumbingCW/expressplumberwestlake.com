What a tankless heater changes

A tank heater keeps a reservoir hot all day. A tankless heater switches on when a hot tap opens, heats the water as it passes through and shuts off when the tap closes. Hot water lasts as long as the unit can keep up with the flow, and the unit takes up much less space.

The trade-off is capacity and setup. A tankless unit has a maximum flow, so running several showers at once can exceed it. It also needs the right gas line and vent or a heavy electrical supply.

Gas line and venting come first

Most gas tankless heaters need a larger gas line than a tank heater, and many need a specific vent type. In an older house, this can mean upgrading the supply from the meter or running new piping in the basement. We check the demand of the other gas appliances in the house before recommending a unit.

Electric tankless units draw heavy current and need dedicated circuits. We look at the panel before offering them, and we say plainly when the house is not set up for it.

Scale and Westlake water

Cleveland Water's 2025 report lists hardness, measured as calcium carbonate, between 105 and 130 with an average of 117. That is moderately hard water, and scale is the main long-term enemy of a tankless heat exchanger. Left alone, it narrows the passages inside and cuts efficiency.

A descaling flush, usually done by circulating a mild acid solution through the unit, removes it. Isolation valves installed with the unit make a flush simple. Ask us about a water softener if you see heavy scale on fixtures.

Hot water that takes too long to arrive

A tankless unit does not shorten the wait caused by a long pipe run. If a bathroom is far from the heater, the water in the pipe has to clear before hot water reaches the tap. A small recirculating pump or a return line fixes that. Our other pages on planned installs and plumbing installation cover the pipe work involved.

When repair beats replacement

Many tankless faults come from sensors, flow switches, igniters or clogged inlet screens, which are repairable. A scaled heat exchanger can often be cleaned. If the heat exchanger itself has failed, replacement of the unit is the better path. We tell you which side of that line your unit is on.

A quick list before you decide

  • Check the gas meter and line size, or the panel capacity for an electric unit.
  • Count the fixtures that run at the same time during your busiest hour.
  • Decide where the vent can exit the house.
  • Plan isolation valves so yearly flushing is easy.

A tankless unit also needs condensate drainage if it is a high-efficiency model. The condensate is mildly acidic, so it usually goes through a neutralizer to a drain. We plan that route during the survey.

A tankless install is governed by the Ohio Plumbing Code, based on the International Plumbing Code (IPC), and by the maker's instructions for gas, venting and condensate.

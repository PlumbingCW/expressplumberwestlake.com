When replacement beats repair

Some water heaters cannot be saved. A tank that leaks from the shell has corroded through, and no part fixes that. Heavy rust at the base, repeated element or valve failures and a unit that no longer keeps up with the household all point toward replacement.

We replace tank heaters like for like in most cases, and we also help with upgrades to larger tanks, hybrid models and tankless units when the home supports them.

Tank failed or flooding: what to do first

Cut off the cold water to the heater, kill the power at the breaker for electric units or set the gas control to off, and open a hot faucet to relieve pressure. Move stored items off the floor and watch for water headed to a floor drain.

Choosing the right size and fuel

Fuel type usually stays the same unless you are changing the home's setup. Capacity depends on how many people use hot water and at what times. A household that runs a shower, a dishwasher and a washer in the same hour needs more recovery than a couple that spreads its use out.

We ask about your household and the existing unit before recommending a size. For a planned upgrade rather than an emergency, see water heater installation.

Venting and combustion air matter as much as capacity. A new gas tank must be vented correctly, and the room must have enough air for the burner. Those checks are part of every gas swap.

Extending the life of the new heater

Cleveland Water's 2025 report lists moderately hard water, with hardness averaging 117 as calcium carbonate. That means scale will collect in the new tank over time. A yearly flush and an occasional anode check are the cheapest way to get a full life out of the heater. If scale is a recurring problem at your fixtures, a water softener is worth testing for.

Access, the old tank and what to clear beforehand

Basement stairs, tight utility rooms and low ceilings affect how a tank is moved out and a new one set. Clear a path from the stairs to the heater and move storage away from the unit. If anyone in the house uses a medical device that needs steady power, tell dispatch so we plan around it.

Many systems also need an expansion tank or new shutoff valves when a tank is replaced. We tell you what the install needs before work starts and show you the parts that go in.

A replacement should follow the manufacturer's instructions and the Ohio Plumbing Code, which the Ohio Board of Building Standards adopted on the basis of the International Plumbing Code (IPC). Ask your plumber to confirm the relief valve and its discharge pipe meet it.
